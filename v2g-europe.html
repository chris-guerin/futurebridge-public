FutureBridge Signals · Vehicle-to-grid · August 2026

Europe is fixing vehicle-to-grid. The open question is who gets to dispatch it.

For a decade the business case died on the same point: the same electron was charged network fees twice. Germany removed that in November 2025.1 From 1 January 2027 every newly installed or refurbished EU charge point must support the protocol that carries bidirectional power.6 The barriers are coming down.

Which makes “does V2G work?” the wrong question to be asking. On Octopus’ own published figures, smart one-way charging already saves the household more than their V2G tariff does.20 The incremental value of reverse flow is not a lower bill. It is €6.4 billion a year of balancing-market revenue21 — and that accrues to whoever holds market access and dispatch control, not to whoever built the car.

01The barrier

It was never the technology

Bidirectional hardware has worked since the CHAdeMO Leaf. The arithmetic did not. In most European markets electricity drawn into a car battery paid network charges, and the same electricity paid again on the way out — a levy on an asset doing the grid a service.

On 13 November 2025 the Bundestag amended the Energy Industry Act to end double network charging and treat electricity fed back from an EV battery as storage electricity.1 The Bundesnetzagentur’s MiSpeL determination operationalises it from 1 October 2026, with a transitional period to 30 September 2027 during which it applies only by agreement with the grid and metering operator.23

The economics did not improve. The penalty was removed.

03The counterfactual

The comparison is not V2G against nothing

Almost every V2G figure in circulation is quoted against a standard variable tariff. That is the wrong baseline. The household’s real alternative is intelligent one-way charging — already mass market, already cheap, already installed.

Octopus publishes both. Over May 2025 to April 2026, customers on Intelligent Octopus Go — a V1G tariff — typically saved £771 against Flexible Octopus.20 Power Pack, the V2G product, is quoted at around £620 against a standard variable tariff, and up to £850 on Octopus’ own modelling.4

Power Pack — vehicle-to-grid, quoted range£620–850
Solid bar is the reported £620; the paler extension reaches Octopus’ modelled £850.

These are not like-for-like. Different periods, baselines and usage assumptions; Power Pack is an add-on alongside a home tariff and bundles free charging, Intelligent Octopus Go is a full tariff. We are not claiming V1G beats V2G by £151. We are pointing out that the two numbers are the same order of magnitude — and that no public V2G figure we found is quoted against a smart-charging baseline rather than a standard one.

The system-level literature says the same thing more precisely. Modelling European charging infrastructure and the energy system together, V1G captures the majority of system cost savings — €19–42 billion a year — while V2G adds incremental system savings of up to €2.5 billion. But V2G generates €6.4 billion a year in balancing market revenues.21

V2G — incremental system cost savings€2.5bn
V2G — balancing market revenues€6.4bn

V2G’s incremental value is not a cheaper bill. It is a trading position.

That single fact reorganises the whole market. If the extra value of reverse flow shows up as balancing revenue rather than consumer savings, it is captured by whoever can register in the market, forecast the pool and dispatch it — not by whoever manufactured the battery. An OEM selling bidirectional capability as a vehicle feature is selling an input to someone else’s trading book.

It also explains the physics of why anyone bothers. Smart charging moves a connection between +7 kW and 0 kW. Bidirectional moves it between +7 kW and −7 kW — a 14 kW controllable swing on the same connection. The incremental grid asset is real and large. The incremental consumer asset is close to nil. Those two facts together are the story.

04Value pools

“Who captures the margin” is the wrong question

There is no single pot. There are at least nine, and they are separable — which is why BMW and E.ON can both win from the same customer.

Pool 01
Energy procurement arbitrage
Buying low, storing, using high. Needs a supply licence and a book.
Pool 02
Balancing & flexibility revenue
The €6.4bn pool. Needs market registration and dispatch.
Pool 03
Tariff margin
The spread on supplying the household at all. Retailer.
Pool 04
Charger hardware margin
Wallbox and installation. OEM, retailer or CPO — genuinely contested.
Pool 05
Software & platform fees
Optimisation sold to whoever owns the customer. Neutral aggregator.
Pool 06
Customer acquisition value
A bundled V2G offer acquires an energy customer cheaply. Retailer.
Pool 07
Vehicle differentiation
Closes a sale. OEM — and it erodes as capability becomes standard.
Pool 08
Residual value
A battery with a demonstrated second income may hold value — or lose it to cycling. Captive finance.
Pool 09
Retention
A household earning from its car changes supplier less. Whoever holds the contract.

Read that way, the live European partnerships stop looking like a single race. BMW with E.ON, Ford with Octopus, Kia and Hyundai in the Netherlands, Renault with its own wallbox, Volkswagen through Elli:5 each is a different split of nine pools, and several are plausibly positive for both parties. We do not know the commercial terms of any of them — they are not public, and this paper does not guess at them.

What is observable is posture. Volkswagen through Elli is attempting to hold more pools in-house. Ford with Octopus is the opposite trade: concede the electricity pools, keep the vehicle. Both are coherent. They imply completely different answers to the section that follows.

05The driveway

When does V2G escape the driveway?

Every retail V2G saving quoted in this paper assumes a car parked for long predictable hours on private property at a charger the household controls. Power Pack asks for 12 hours a day, 20 days a month.17 That is a duty obligation, not a tariff preference.

In the UK, roughly 35% of households — about 9.6 million — have no off-street parking,13 and around 90% of on-street households outside London are not within a five-minute walk of a public charge point.14

It would be easy, and wrong, to conclude from that that V2G is structurally a driveway product. Utrecht already runs bidirectional car-sharing on public infrastructure.5 The driveway is not the ceiling.

The driveway is the current boundary of the commercially addressable retail market. The strategic question is when it moves.

Today
Home charger, private driveway. Long dwell, one owner, one meter. The only place retail V2G economics presently close.

Whoever owns identity

The mandate creates the handshake, not the hardware

The 1 January 2027 AFIR requirement is widely read as making public charge points V2G-capable. It requires support for EN ISO 15118-20:20226 — the communication standard whose scope includes bidirectional transfer. That is the conversation. Not the inverter, not export metering, not the settlement arrangement that lets a lamppost buy power back from a car.

We could find no UK legislation requiring bidirectional capability on on-street or lamppost charge points, and the widely repeated claim of a March 2025 UK obligation above 22 kW is contradicted between secondary sources; we do not rely on it.15 Hardware is also not yet fungible — CHAdeMO and CCS split the base, and the strongest products are deliberately closed: Enphase around IQ8, SolarEdge around its DC bus, GM Energy.16

So the question worth funding is: who pays for the escape, and who captures it? Kerbside export hardware benefits the distribution network, the aggregator and the on-street household — three parties, none of whom currently holds the investment case alone. That is a policy gap, and it is the single largest swing factor in this paper.

06The churn

Churn is a cost line, not an existential threat

Roughly four in five UK new-car finance deals are PCP, on two-to-four year terms, and only about 15–20% of customers settle the final payment and keep the vehicle.18 A retail V2G portfolio therefore turns over most of its vehicles inside four years.

It is tempting to call that fatal. It is not. An aggregator does not need a particular car for four years; it needs enough qualifying batteries in the pool. One leaves, another joins. A portfolio of a million vehicles absorbs very large individual churn provided replacement rates are predictable. That is what aggregation is for.

The asymmetry is the point, not the churn.

Churn is neutral for smart charging and negative-selecting for V2G. Any replacement EV rejoins a V1G portfolio. Only a bidirectional replacement rejoins a V2G one — and on current model availability most replacements will not be. So the right hypothesis is not that churn breaks contracts. It is:

Does vehicle churn materially raise V2G acquisition, requalification and hardware-friction cost per delivered MW, relative to V1G?

That is measurable, and it resolves to one observable: the V2G-to-V2G replacement rate. It sits below 100% until bidirectional is standard, and every point below 100% is a re-acquisition the V1G competitor does not pay for.

We searched for published Power Pack term length, transferability and vehicle-change provisions and found none.19 We record that as an absence, not as a claim about the terms. It is the first thing to ask for when underwriting a portfolio.

07The layer

Nobody is winning Europe. Several are winning the right to be the layer everyone else rents.

Electricity markets are national. Balancing products, network tariffs, metering rules and settlement all stop at the border, and the German rules are not unconditional until late 2027.2 So the pan-European players are not competing for a European market. They are competing to amortise one platform across many national markets faster than a national incumbent can build its own.

And if the barriers keep falling, bidirectional capability itself commoditises. Once most OEMs support ISO 15118-20 and compatible hardware, having a V2G-capable car is not differentiation. The contest moves up a layer, to five questions that have nothing to do with vehicles:

Who owns the customer? Who controls dispatch? Who aggregates the batteries? Who holds market access? And who can see the household’s other flexible assets?

That last one is the asymmetry an OEM cannot fix by building a better car. Volkswagen through Elli can see a 70 kWh car. A domestic energy supplier can see the car plus a home battery, solar, a heat pump, and the household load curve. It optimises across all of them.

Which means the OEM’s asset does not merely fail to earn. It gets ranked.

An optimiser holding a home battery and a car will cycle the cheapest asset first — and a home battery has no warranty exposure, no degradation argument with a manufacturer, and no risk of driving away at 6pm. Inside someone else’s stack, the vehicle is the marginal asset, dispatched last and earning least. The OEM does not get to see that happening.

Three postures are visible today. The vertically integrated retailer — owns customer, tariff, optimisation and increasingly the OEM partnerships, and exports the stack; constrained by having to win retail customers market by market. The neutral aggregator — The Mobility House, Jedlix, Virta, Nuvve — sells market access to whoever owns the customer, scales across borders more easily precisely because it does not need the retail relationship, and captures less per vehicle for the same reason. Worth noting how early this still is: Nuvve’s revenue, up 268% year on year, remains driven principally by selling V2G hardware rather than by traded flexibility.7 The utility incumbent — strong where it already holds the meter, weak where it does not.

For a carmaker the strategic question is therefore not whether V2G works. It is whether the answer to “who dispatches this battery” is settled before it has decided which pools in section 04 it intends to hold.

08Take-up

The parc arithmetic, whoever wins

The common reference is that 30% of EVs are V2G-capable by 2030.8 Europe’s passenger EV parc is projected past 40 million by 20309 — roughly twelve million capable cars. Against that: of 146 EV models scheduled for 2024 release, four supported ISO 15118-20 Plug&Charge,10 and T&E proposes mandatory type approval only by 203211 — two years after the date the figure is set against.

09What to watch

Six things that would tell you the answer early

Written as observable thresholds rather than opinions, so the evidence can settle them.

Public V2G
First European market with more than 10,000 genuinely public or kerbside bidirectional connections

Not chargers that speak ISO 15118-20 — connections that can actually export and settle. The moment the driveway boundary in section 05 moves.

Vehicle penetration
More than 25% of new BEVs sold in Europe are bidirectional-capable

The parc arithmetic in section 08 is entirely governed by this number. Today it is close to nil.

Interoperability
First third-party supplier commercially controlling V2G across three or more unrelated OEMs on non-proprietary hardware

The test of whether capability commoditises. Until it happens, every deployment is a closed pairing and the layer thesis is unproven.

V1G / V2G economics
Reverse-flow flexibility earns more than twice the incremental cost of enabling V2G over smart charging

The question section 03 raises and cannot yet answer. Until this clears, V2G is a balancing-revenue play subsidised by a consumer proposition that V1G already delivers.

Aggregation
First 1 GW commercially dispatchable multi-OEM V2G portfolio

The scale at which the orchestration layer becomes a real asset rather than a pilot. Watch who announces it, and whether they build cars.

OEM disintermediation
An independent retailer or aggregator controls more V2G-connected vehicles than any OEM-owned energy platform

The falsification of the OEM-led model. If it happens before the parc reaches scale, the carmakers will have supplied the asset and rented the layer.
