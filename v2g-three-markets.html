FutureBridge Signals · V2G in three markets · August 2026

Three markets that could decide whether V2G matters.

Britain could prove it.Europe may need it.India could make it worth far more.

Vehicle-to-grid keeps being judged as a consumer arbitrage product and keeps testing badly. That is because the interesting question is not whether a motorist earns enough. It is whether anyone can make V2G work for the drivers who have nowhere to plug in — and what happens to two much larger markets if somebody does.

These three are not independent cases. They are a chain. Britain is where a mass-market model beyond the driveway could be proved. Europe is where a proved model would immediately matter, because its own storage plan is short and its own V2G is fragmented. And India is where the same capability would be worth the most, because it is the one large market with no incumbent passenger EV answer at all. China runs through all three — not as a threat, but as the party with the fewest reasons not to try.

01The chain

One story, read in order

Each link is a question the next link depends on. That dependency is the argument; the individual markets are only the evidence.

BritainCould prove it
Can anyone make V2G work beyond the driveway — at mass-market scale, for cars people own rather than share?
About a third of British households have no off-street parking. Every commercial V2G product on sale assumes a private charger. Britain has the on-street population, the market access and the electricity markets to settle this, and it is installing the kerbside estate right now.
↓
EuropeMay need it
Can Europe’s fragmented, home-grown V2G reach scale before a proven model next door makes importing one irresistible?
The EU needs 200 GW of storage by 2030 and its own medium scenario reaches about 160. Its V2G is real, well-engineered, national, OEM-locked and measured in hundreds of vehicles. The requirement is not.
↓
IndiaCould make it worth far more
Could an integrated car-plus-charger-plus-backup proposition enter the one big market with no incumbent answer — and be worth more there than anywhere?
India is electrifying around batteries too small to matter nationally, while needing 208 GWh of grid storage and facing the steepest evening ramp of the three. That leaves an unusually empty strategic space for the car.

Britain is the laboratory. Europe is the market. India is the prize.

Method: each case is stated at its strongest, then attacked. The attack is the point — a case that survives a serious attempt to break it is worth acting on; one that has never been attacked is an advocacy position. Verdicts are survives, relocates (holds, but the constraint is not where it is usually placed) and not proven (the case rests on something that has not happened, and we say so).

02Could prove itBritain

The market beyond the driveway

Every V2G product on sale in Europe today is a driveway product. That is not a technology boundary. It is a commercial one, and it strands about a third of the market.

Estimates of British households without off-street parking cluster around a third — roughly 32% in England, about 35% on the RAC Foundation’s figure, some surveys higher — which is on the order of 9 to 10 million households.1 Whatever the precise number, it is not a niche, and it is the part of the market that home-charger economics cannot reach.

No off-street parking
Private charger — BMW/E.ON, VW/Elli, Renault/Mobilize all assume it ~9–10 m UK households, served by a kerbside estate with no bidirectional product

The critical thing is that public V2G is not speculative. It has been proved. Utrecht has run a public bidirectional AC system since June 2025, now scaling to 500 bidirectional Renault 5 E-Techs against 500 bidirectional chargers, past 120,000 charging transactions, and reported as supplying roughly a tenth of the region’s grid flexibility.2 The engineering question is closed. What is open is whether it works for privately owned cars, in a mixed fleet, at a scale past four hundred.

And here is the thing worth noticing about Britain: the kerbside estate is being built right now, and it is being built one-way. char.gy passed its 5,000th public charge point in May 2026, with Brighton & Hove alone planning around 6,000 kerbside sockets — the largest on-street programme outside London — against a national estate of more than 80,000 public chargepoints.3 We could find no bidirectional public on-street charging programme in the UK at all. Kerbside rollouts surfaced in search are unidirectional; the bidirectional UK products are home wallboxes.4

Concrete poured once lasts a decade. This is the window, and it is open now.

Why China is the party most likely to walk through it. China has by mandate the single thing European V2G most conspicuously lacks: one charging architecture across every car it sells at home. GB/T 20234 is mandatory and 100% of China-sold EVs use it, and its Part 3 update carries V2G support.5 Add cheap V2G-capable vehicles, a domestic charging-hardware industry, and a UK market with rapidly rising Chinese share — 15% of new car registrations in H1 20266 — no tariff wall, permissive procurement, and the most sophisticated flexibility markets in Europe.

Attempt to break itNot proven — and one specific thing would have to change
The attackThe premise is that Chinese manufacturers will standardise a common bidirectional architecture across their European vehicles and pair it with cheap public charging. Check whether that is happening.

It is not happening, and this is the correction the case needs. Chinese OEMs have not brought their bidirectional architecture to Europe. In the UK and EU today, only BYD’s Dolphin offers V2G; MG offers vehicle-to-load only; XPeng offers V2L only.7 Worse for the thesis, cars sold into Europe use Type 2 and CCS2, not GB/T — so the mandatory-standard advantage does not automatically travel with the vehicle. The thing that makes China structurally well placed at home is precisely the thing it leaves at the border.

So the strong form of the case fails. What survives is more useful, because it is actionable. The stranded third is real. The public bidirectional technology is proved. The British kerbside estate is being installed unidirectional at thousands of sockets a year. Nobody — Chinese or European — is currently building for this market, and the opportunity is open rather than being taken.

That makes this a live commercial question rather than a prediction. The party best placed to answer it is whoever can put a single bidirectional architecture behind a large, cheap, growing vehicle parc and pair it with kerbside hardware. China has all the ingredients and has not assembled them outside its own market. Europe has assembled them, for driveways only. The first to do both, in Britain, sets the reference design for everyone else.

03May need itEuropean Union

The clock, not the capability

Whether V2G can help Europe is settled. Whether Europe’s own version of it can get big enough, fast enough, is not.

The EU needs about 200 GW of storage by 2030 and holds roughly 55 today; its own medium scenario reaches about 160 GW, and the plan naming the target carries no procurement mechanism, auction framework or mandatory obligation.8 The European passenger EV parc is projected past 40 million cars by 2030.9 The physical headroom is not in question: covering a 40 GW residual at a 7 kW connection needs about 5.7 million cars plugged in and willing at once, roughly 14% of that parc.

What is in question is the delivery vehicle. Europe’s V2G is genuinely good and genuinely small. Set the programmes against the requirement and the gap is not technological.

Bars are to the same scale. Three of the four programmes publish no vehicle count, so no bar is drawn for them — an empty track means undisclosed, not zero. The one published figure is Utrecht’s, and at this scale it is a sliver. That non-disclosure is itself a fair indication of scale.10

Each of these is national, OEM-locked and driveway-shaped. A BMW customer on E.ON in Germany, a Renault customer on Mobilize in France, a Volkswagen customer on Elli from the fourth quarter. They are four good products, not one market. A driver cannot take the proposition across a border or across a brand, and none of them serves a household without a private charger.

Europe may need V2G before its own V2G is ready to supply it.

Attempt to break itSurvives
The attackFragmentation is a normal early-market condition, not a strategic failure. These launched in 2026; judging their scale in 2026 is judging a product on its release date. And Europe has ISO 15118-20, which will deliver interoperability without anyone importing anything.

The timing objection is fair and materially weakens the alarmist reading. These are launches, not plateaus, and a 2028 count is the honest test. Nothing here says European V2G fails.

But the attack does not touch the actual claim, which is about a race rather than a capability. The storage requirement has a date on it. If the residual gap is to be met even partly by vehicles, the fleet has to be interoperable and addressable well before 2030 — which means the reference architecture gets set in the next two or three years, by whoever ships one that works across brands and beyond driveways.

And the standard does less than it is credited with. ISO 15118-20 specifies the communication handshake. It does not mandate a bidirectional inverter, a metering arrangement, a settlement route or a supplier willing to buy — which is why Germany needed a separate legal change before V2G became commercially possible there at all.11 An agreed handshake between parties who have not agreed anything else is not interoperability.

So the case survives, and it is a clock rather than a threat. If a mass-market, brand-agnostic, kerbside-capable model is demonstrated next door around 2028–29, Europe’s choice is not whether to have V2G. It is whether to keep scaling four national products or adopt a proven one — and that is exactly how a supply industry gets decided without anybody choosing.

04Worth far moreIndia

The empty strategic space for the car

India’s EV market is overwhelmingly two- and three-wheelers. That fact is usually offered as the opportunity. It is actually the setup.

India needs roughly 60.6 GW / 336 GWh of storage by 2029–30, of which about 41.7 GW / 208 GWh is batteries.12 The shape of the need is specific: solar has hollowed out the middle of the day and left the evening alone, so the evening net-load ramp has grown from about 27 GW in 2023 to about 65 GW in 2026, and across April–May 2026 the grid fell short on 36 of 61 days at the non-solar peak against 6 of 61 at the solar peak.13 Behind that sits demand growth of about 6.4% a year to 2030, peak demand rising from 289 GW in FY27 toward 388 GW by FY32, and data-centre capacity going from 2.2 GW to 12 GW by 2030.14

First, the answer that does not work

Of 2.3 million EVs sold in India in 2025, two-wheelers were 57% and three-wheelers 35% — 92% of the market — against roughly 175,000 electric cars. The parc is about 5.6 million vehicles.15 At a blended 3.7 kWh pack, the entire Indian EV fleet holds only about 21 GWh — and that is nameplate, before availability or depth of discharge. Against a 208 GWh requirement, and against the ~130 GWh needed to halve a single summer evening’s ramp,13 the two- and three-wheeler fleet is not a national grid resource this decade. It is excellent transport and a fine household backup, and it is not storage policy.

Which is exactly why the space for the car is open.

Then, the answer that might

India sells about 4.7 million passenger vehicles a year, of which only around 200,000 are electric — roughly 4%. The market is expected to reach 6 million by 2030, with the government targeting 30% EV penetration and independent forecasts closer to 20%.16 A car pack is 50–80 kWh: fifteen to twenty times a scooter’s. That changes the arithmetic by an order of magnitude, and nobody owns this market yet.

Model, not forecast. One year of car sales is compared with the national BESS requirement on a nameplate basis — useful for sizing the estate, not a claim that a car fleet substitutes one-for-one for grid batteries. The third figure is the honest deliverable version: one year’s cars, at the availability set here and a 60% usable depth, measured against the ~130 GWh that halving one summer evening’s ramp requires. Defaults are the sourced central case: a 6 m car market, 20% electric, 50 kWh.

The proposition this creates is not a car with a feature. It is an oven-ready energy asset sold with the means to use it — car, bidirectional charger, home backup and a grid contract, in one package. In India that stacks value the same asset cannot stack in Europe: at home it displaces the inverter battery or the generator that the household is already buying, during the solar surplus it absorbs midday generation the grid is currently wasting, and in the evening it discharges into precisely the ramp the system cannot meet.

And the policy version is sharper than the consumer one. India can acquire a large part of a distributed storage estate without procuring it as stationary BESS, because consumers buy the batteries as cars. For a country adding data centres, industrial load and air conditioning at 6.4% a year, that is a materially different proposition from a resilience feature.

Attempt to break itSurvives as strategy, not as forecast
The attackIndia has almost no electric cars, no V2G regulation, a price-obsessed market that rejects expensive vehicles, and this needs a 2030s fleet to matter. Nameplate GWh in cars is not deliverable energy to a grid, and comparing the two flatters the case.

The nameplate objection is correct and is conceded in the model itself. A car fleet is not a substitute for grid batteries: it is unavailable when driven, degrades on cycling, sits behind a distribution network that has to accept export, and delivers a fraction of its nameplate into any given hour. The third output above exists to keep that honest, and it lands in the low single-digit percentages of a single evening’s ramp at central assumptions.

The regulatory objection is also correct, and is the nearest hard blocker. Vehicle-to-load and limited vehicle-to-home are permitted in India; grid export is not. The Ministry of Power asked the CEA to frame reverse-charging guidelines in March 2023 and the grid code still needs amending.17 Nothing at grid scale happens until that is done, and the home half does not need it.

What the attack does not touch is the strategic claim, which is about who gets there first. India’s passenger EV market is 4% electric and has no incumbent V2G proposition of any kind. The battery estate arrives over the 2030s whether or not anyone designs for it — the only question is whether it arrives bidirectional. That is a specification decision being made now, in vehicles and chargers being homologated now, exactly as the kerbside decision is being made now in Britain. The cost of building it in is small; the cost of retrofitting a national parc is not.

Which is why India is last in the chain rather than first. It has the largest prize and the least proof. It is the market that most rewards a model somebody else has already demonstrated — and Britain is where that demonstration is cheapest.

06What would tell us early

Six observable things, and what each would settle

Every one is public, dated and checkable. Several would break something written above.

Britain · the strongest single marker
Any UK local authority specifies bidirectional capability in a kerbside charging tender

Nothing else would move as fast. LEVI-funded procurement is where the one-way decision is currently being made by default rather than on the merits.

Britain · would restore the strong case
A second Chinese OEM ships a V2G-capable car into the UK or EU

Today only BYD’s Dolphin qualifies, which is why section 02 reads “not proven”. Two or three would make the common-architecture thesis live rather than hypothetical.

Europe · the fragmentation test
Any European V2G programme passes 50,000 vehicles, or publishes a number at all

Three of the four named programmes do not disclose fleet size. Disclosure would settle whether section 03’s clock is running fast or slow.

Europe · would break the OEM-lock reading
One V2G tariff accepts vehicles from three or more unrelated manufacturers

The single cleanest test of whether interoperability is real or merely standardised on paper.

India · the hard blocker
CEA reverse-charging guidelines are notified and the grid code amended

Asked for in March 2023 and still outstanding. Until it lands, everything in section 04 above the household meter is theory.

India · the specification decision
Any manufacturer homologates a bidirectional passenger car for the Indian market

Cheap to build in now, expensive to retrofit later. The first one to do it defines what “an Indian EV” includes.
