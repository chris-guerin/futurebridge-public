FutureBridge Signals · V2G in three markets · August 2026

Three reasons V2G might matter — and none of them is selling electricity from your car.

Judged as a consumer arbitrage product — buy low, sell high, from a car on a driveway — vehicle-to-grid looks marginal almost everywhere. That judgement has been made repeatedly, and it keeps being made about the wrong thing.

V2G is not one technology looking for one business case. It is one capability that answers a different question in each market. In Britain the question is who ends up controlling a battery estate somebody else manufactures. In the EU it is whether a fleet nobody had to procure can close a storage gap policy is going to miss. In India it is whether a household should have to buy a battery twice. We took all three and tried to break them.

01The frame

One capability, three problems

The same hardware. Three different reasons a country might want it, three different beneficiaries, three different things that could stop it.

United Kingdom · China
If China supplies an increasing share of Britain’s mobile batteries, who controls and monetises them?
An industrial-strategy question. The battery estate and the right to dispatch it are separable, and they are being separated now.
Control
European Union
If Europe is going to miss its stationary-storage target, how much of the gap could the EV fleet realistically close?
An energy-policy question. The fleet is being bought by motorists whether or not any energy ministry counts it.
Capacity
India
Could the battery bought to move the vehicle replace the battery bought to keep the lights on?
An economic-development question. The comparison is not against the grid. It is against a lead-acid box in the hallway.
Resilience

The technology is identical. The reason to want it is not.

That distinction matters commercially, because each version has a different buyer, a different willingness to pay and a different regulator. A single “V2G business case” built for one of them will be wrong in the other two — which is roughly what has happened to the category so far.

Method: each case below is stated at its strongest, then attacked. The attack is the point. A case that survives a serious attempt to break it is worth acting on; one that has never been attacked is an advocacy position. Verdicts are survives, relocates (the case holds but the constraint is somewhere other than where it is usually placed) and breaks.

02The control caseUK · China

Who captures it?

Britain is not losing a battery industry it had. The open question is whether it keeps the layer that decides what those batteries are worth.

The supply position is not in doubt. Chinese-owned brands took 15% of UK new car registrations in the first half of 2026 — almost 175,000 cars out of 1.13 million — having roughly doubled year on year while the market as a whole grew 9%.1 Upstream the concentration is sharper still: seven Chinese firms in the global top ten accounted for 72.4% of EV battery installations in the same period, CATL alone at 39.9%.2

Read quickly, that looks like the answer. It is not, because the battery and the right to dispatch it are different assets, and in Britain they are held by different people. Dispatch requires a supply licence or an aggregator agreement, a customer relationship, and a position in the balancing market — none of which comes with the vehicle. Britain is unusually strong here: Kraken alone dispatches about 2 GW across 500,000 devices, the largest residential virtual power plant in the world.3

Attempt to break itRelocates
The attackManufacturing share is not control. If the dispatch layer is licensed, British-held and already at scale, then Chinese supply of the physical estate is commercially uninteresting — a component sale into somebody else’s book.

That attack largely succeeds, and it kills the version of this argument usually made. Owning the cell does not confer the right to dispatch the car. Whoever holds the licence and the customer holds the revenue, and today that is overwhelmingly not the vehicle manufacturer.

But something survives the attack, and it is narrower and more interesting than the thing it replaces. A car exports only if its manufacturer permits it to — in firmware, in the warranty, and in whatever it does or does not implement of the bidirectional standard. That permission sits upstream of every licence. Britain’s only live retail V2G product is a leased BYD Dolphin, described as the first UK-market car with native V2G compatibility, sold in a bundle with a British supplier’s tariff and charger.4

So the case relocates. The contested layer is not the cell and not the licence. It is the gateway between them: who decides whether a given car is allowed to be a grid asset at all. Nobody has proposed regulating it, and the absence of any type-approval condition on dispatch access is the part of this worth watching.

03The capacity caseEuropean Union

Does Europe actually need it?

A target that will be missed, and a fleet that is being bought anyway.

The EU needs about 200 GW of storage by 2030 and holds roughly 55 today; its own medium scenario reaches about 160 GW, and the plan that names the target carries no procurement mechanism, auction framework or mandatory obligation.5 Call the residual gap 40 GW. Meanwhile the European passenger EV parc is projected past 40 million cars by 2030, bought by motorists, financed privately, and counted in nobody’s capacity plan.6

The arithmetic is not close. Forty million cars at a 7 kW bidirectional connection is 280 GW of nameplate power. To cover a 40 GW gap you need about 5.7 million cars plugged in, capable and willing at the same moment — roughly 14% of the parc. Unlike almost everything else in this field, that is not a demanding number.

Attempt to break itSurvives
The attackNameplate power is not deliverable power. Cars are driven, unplugged, parked away from home and at low state of charge; a fleet-wide 14% simultaneity is a spreadsheet artefact, and stationary storage is cheaper per delivered kWh precisely because none of that applies to it.

The physical half of the attack fails. Fourteen per cent simultaneity is well inside what a domestic parc plausibly delivers overnight, and the evening peak Europe needs to cover coincides with the hours cars are at home. The headroom against the requirement is roughly sevenfold, which absorbs a great deal of pessimism about availability before the case is in trouble.

The economic half lands, and should be conceded. Stationary storage is a better product for this job on every axis except one: somebody else has already paid for the car. The EV fleet is not cheaper per kWh delivered. It is cheaper per kWh procured, because its capital cost sits on household balance sheets and appears in no energy budget. For a bloc that has set a target and attached no money to it, that distinction is the whole point.

What genuinely constrains this case is neither physics nor cost. It is permission and interoperability — whether the car, the charger, the meter and the supplier can agree, and whether the driver is allowed to sell. Germany removed one of the larger legal barriers to bidirectional charging only recently.7 The case survives on its merits and is held up by paperwork.

04The resilience caseIndia

Why should a household buy storage twice?

India is the only one of the three markets where the battery has a second buyer already, and the only one where the EV is usually not a car.

India needs roughly 60.6 GW / 336 GWh of storage by 2029–30, of which about 41.7 GW / 208 GWh is batteries.8 The shape of the need is unusually specific: solar has hollowed out the middle of the day and left the evening untouched, so the evening net-load ramp has grown from around 27 GW in 2023 to about 65 GW in 2026, and across April–May 2026 the grid fell short on 36 of 61 days at the non-solar peak against 6 of 61 at the solar peak.9

Alongside that sits something Europe does not have: a household backup market that already exists and is already paid for. Diesel gensets are a $1.33bn-a-year market in India, 60% of it standby duty, with residential the fastest-growing segment;10 the home inverter-battery market is about $1.02bn a year on its own.11 Official supply continuity is 23.4 hours a day urban and 22.6 rural, and household surveys report outages far more widely than that implies.12

And India’s EV fleet is not a fleet of cars. Of 2.3 million EVs sold in 2025, two-wheelers were 57% and three-wheelers 35% — 92% of the market — against roughly 175,000 electric cars.13 The parc is about 5.6 million vehicles.14 So the relevant battery is not a 60 kWh car pack. It is a 3.4–4 kWh scooter pack15 or a 5–6 kWh three-wheeler pack.16

Which is still several times the battery the same household already owns.

A standard Indian home inverter battery is 150 Ah at 12 V: 1.8 kWh nominal, and about 0.9 kWh usable, because lead-acid is damaged below roughly 50% depth of discharge. Trade guides put it at ₹14,000–20,000, replaced every three to four years.17

Household side compares usable vehicle energy against 0.9 kWh usable from a standard inverter battery, and converts to hours at a 200 W load — a few fans, lights and a television. Grid side multiplies the parc by pack, availability and depth, and measures it against the ~130 GWh of discharge needed to halve a single summer evening’s ramp between 1pm and 8pm.9 Defaults are the sourced figures: a 5.6 m parc and a 3.7 kWh pack — a two-wheeler, not a car. Push depth and availability to their maxima and the grid answer still does not reach a fifth.

Attempt to break itBreaks — then survives somewhere else
The attackIndia’s EV fleet is far too small and far too fragmented to matter to the grid, its batteries are in use exactly when the evening peak arrives, and exporting to the grid is not even legal yet. The two-birds story is arithmetic that does not close.

Against the grid, the attack succeeds completely, and the ceiling is what proves it. Take the entire 5.6 million-vehicle parc, at a blended 4.5 kWh, at a physically impossible 100% availability and 100% depth of discharge: roughly 25 GWh, against the ~130 GWh needed to halve one summer evening’s ramp. Under a fifth of one evening’s job, at settings no fleet can deliver. At plausible settings it is low single-digit percentages. India’s EV fleet is not a grid resource this decade, and the regulatory position agrees: V2L and limited vehicle-to-home are permitted, but grid export awaits guidelines the Ministry of Power asked the CEA to draft in 2023 and a change to the grid code.18

Against the household, the same attack fails outright, because the comparison is not the grid. It is 0.9 kWh of usable lead-acid. A single scooter pack at a conservative 40% depth releases roughly 1.5 kWh — about 1.6 times the battery the household already owns, and it is bought for another reason entirely. That case needs no aggregator, no balancing market, no tariff and no grid code amendment, because the electricity never leaves the property.

And here is the part that inverts the usual complaint about India. The blocker is not regulation — vehicle-to-load is already allowed. It is that the product does not exist. V2L in India is confined to a handful of imported cars; no mass-market Indian two-wheeler ships with an export socket.19 Meanwhile swap networks have already made the battery portable and household-charged: packs weigh about 13 kg, and some providers’ batteries can be charged by drivers at home from an ordinary socket.20 The energy already flows from the house into the battery. Making it flow back is a socket and an inverter.

One more thing worth naming, because it is the largest dispatchable battery estate in the country and nobody can size it. Battery-swap networks hold their packs in grid-connected racks, aggregated, behind a commercial meter, charging on a schedule an operator already controls — which is most of what a virtual power plant is. Battery Smart alone runs more than 1,600 stations across 50-plus cities, has passed 100 million swaps, serves over 100,000 drivers and is reckoned to be about 70% of India’s swapping infrastructure;21 SUN Mobility runs 900-plus stations and 1.4 million swaps a month.22 The aggregate energy sitting in those racks is not publicly disclosed by any operator we could find — which is itself the finding. India’s most controllable battery estate cannot be sized from public data, and it is not counted in any storage number in this piece.

06What this tells us

The economics depend on what the country needs the battery to do

Put the three attacks next to one another and the pattern is not about batteries at all.

United Kingdom
The constraint is a commercial right

Not manufacturing share. Whoever decides whether a car may export — upstream of every supply licence — holds the position, and nobody regulates that decision today.

European Union
The constraint is permission

The arithmetic clears its requirement about sevenfold. What is missing is the right to act on it: interoperability, metering, and whether the driver may sell.

India
The constraint is a socket

Vehicle-to-load is already legal, the battery is already portable and already charged at home, and the thing it would displace costs ₹14,000–20,000 every three or four years. No manufacturer ships the port.

In all three markets the binding constraint is permission, not physics.

That is why the standalone consumer proposition keeps testing badly. Asked whether a motorist will buy a car to earn arbitrage revenue from its battery, the answer is decidedly marginal, and every market returns roughly the same disappointing number. Asked what problem the battery could solve that somebody is already paying to solve another way, the answers diverge completely — and two of the three are large.

So the conclusion is not that V2G works, and not that it does not. It is that V2G is infrastructure whose value is set by the scarcity local to it. In Britain the scarce thing is control. In the EU it is capacity nobody has budgeted for. In India it is a household’s second battery — and there, the version that survives contact with the evidence is the one that never touches the grid at all.

What would change these conclusions, in order of how much they would change: an Indian two-wheeler manufacturer shipping an export socket as standard; a type-approval or licence condition anywhere attaching dispatch access to vehicle sale; a swap operator publishing its aggregate connected energy; or an EU member state making bidirectional metering the default rather than an application. The first is a product decision, the second and third are disclosure, and only the fourth is energy policy.
