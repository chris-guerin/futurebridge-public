FutureBridge Signals · Vehicle-to-grid · August 2026

Three markets that could decide whether V2G matters.

Vehicle-to-grid has spent most of a decade being assessed as a consumer product: whether a motorist earns enough selling electricity back from a parked car. Judged principally as a way for motorists to buy electricity cheaply and sell it back later, the economics have often looked surprisingly marginal. The more interesting questions are being asked somewhere else.

Three markets expose different parts of the same question. Britain could show whether V2G can escape the driveway. Europe will show whether home-grown solutions can scale quickly enough to matter. India shows what happens when the value of the battery extends well beyond electricity trading.

China sits awkwardly across all three. It has the cars, the batteries, the charging hardware and the domestic standardisation to assemble a complete proposition, but has not yet done so outside China. Britain may be the easiest place to find out what happens if it does.

01Britain

The market beyond the driveway

Britain is spending heavily to build charging for people who have nowhere to park a car of their own — and installing almost all of it one-way.

Europe’s emerging retail V2G products have overwhelmingly been designed around a predictable private connection: a car returning to the same charger at a home or a depot. That leaves an awkward question for Britain, where roughly one in three households — around 9 to 10 million — has no off-street parking.1

It is not a niche. Nor is the underlying ability to charge bidirectionally the unresolved technology problem. The problem is making it work commercially and interoperably in a place where neither the car nor the parking space is controlled.

No off-street parking

What Utrecht settled, and what it did not

Utrecht has been running public bidirectional AC charging since June 2025, now scaling to 500 bidirectional Renault 5 E-Techs against 500 bidirectional chargers, past 120,000 charging transactions and reported as supplying roughly a tenth of the region’s grid flexibility.2

That has removed one important uncertainty: public bidirectional charging is no longer a laboratory proposition. The harder question is whether it can work with privately owned cars, mixed brands and ordinary kerbside parking — where dwell times are unpredictable, the vehicle mix is whatever people happen to have bought, and nobody controls the fleet. Utrecht is a managed car-sharing operation. That is a demonstrator, and a good one, rather than a market.

Two infrastructure decisions, made separately

Meanwhile the kerbside estate is going in. char.gy passed its 5,000th public charge point in May 2026, with Brighton & Hove alone planning around 6,000 sockets — the largest on-street programme outside London — and West Northamptonshire commissioning more than 3,000 lamp-column retrofits, explicitly with no excavation and no new grid connection, against a national public estate above 80,000 chargepoints.3

What we did not find

Against 9 to 10 million UK households with no off-street parking, and thousands of kerbside chargepoints being specified and installed right now:

  • No bidirectional public on-street charging programme anywhere in the UK.

Every kerbside rollout we examined is one-way; the bidirectional products on sale in Britain are home wallboxes. Stated as an absence rather than an established zero — one announced trial would overturn it. Note also that a lamp-column retrofit taking no new grid connection is one-way by design.4

Britain is therefore making two infrastructure decisions at once: how millions of cars without driveways will charge, and whether those connections will ever be able to work in reverse. Today, those decisions are largely being made separately.

The significance is timing rather than permanence. Thousands of kerbside connections are being specified now. Retrofitting bidirectionality later will almost certainly be harder and more expensive than deciding whether to accommodate it while the estate is being built.

Where China actually has an advantage

It is tempting to look at Chinese market share — 15% of UK new car registrations in the first half of 2026, almost 175,000 cars, up 121% year on year6 — and conclude that the answer will simply arrive from China. The reality is more particular, and worth separating carefully.

China’s advantage is not that it can export its charging standard to Britain. It cannot. Chinese cars sold in Europe conform to European charging interfaces — Type 2 and CCS2, not GB/T.7 Nor does a common connector amount to a working V2G market: the physical interface, the communication protocol and a commercially interoperable bidirectional service are three different things, and only the first is settled by a plug.

The advantage is institutional. China has already demonstrated that it can impose common technical requirements across an enormous vehicle and charging market: GB/T 20234 is mandatory, effectively all China-sold EVs use it, and its Part 3 update carries V2G support.5 If Chinese manufacturers chose to converge on a single European-compatible bidirectional implementation, they would begin with unusual scale in vehicles, cells and charger manufacturing, and experience operating within a nationally standardised charging architecture.

There is an obvious objection, however: nothing we found shows Chinese manufacturers doing this today. Britain has a growing list of V2G-capable cars — Nissan, Renault, Kia, Hyundai and Volkswagen models among them — but among the Chinese brands only BYD’s Dolphin appears on it. MG offers vehicle-to-load only, and XPeng offers V2L only.7 BYD is the important exception rather than evidence of a coordinated Chinese strategy. The ingredients exist; the dish has not been cooked.

Nobody is currently building a V2G proposition for the third of the market that has nowhere private to plug in.

02Europe

Europe has V2G products. It does not yet have a V2G market.

That distinction decides whether any of it scales, and it is the one the deployment numbers actually reveal.

The EU reaffirmed a 200 GW energy storage ambition for 2030 in the final Electrification Action Plan published in July 2026, without attaching a dedicated financing mechanism to it.8 How far short the bloc will fall is genuinely contested, and worth stating rather than resolving: SolarPower Europe puts the medium scenario at about 160 GW, while Energy Storage Europe and LCP Delta forecast European capacity above 215 GW by 2030. Europe passed 102.7 GW installed in 2025 — 53.3 GW of pumped hydro and 48.7 GW of electrochemical storage.9

Those figures are not like for like: they vary by geography (EU against Europe), by technology included, and between power and energy. We have therefore not calculated a residual gap from them, and nothing below depends on one. The relevant point for this article is simply that the target is large, contested, and unfunded.

For scale, and only for scale: 5.7 million cars exporting 7 kW simultaneously would provide 40 GW of power — about a fifth of the 2030 ambition, from roughly 14% of the 40 million-car parc Europe is projected to have by then.10 That does not make 5.7 million cars equivalent to 40 GW of stationary storage. Duration, availability, state of charge and network location all matter, and a storage target is not simply a power number. But it does demonstrate that the mobile estate is large enough to be relevant.

Products inside controlled stacks

A product works inside a controlled stack. A market exists when a driver can change car, energy supplier or country and expect the same battery to remain dispatchable. Europe has demonstrated the first and not the second.

Bars are to one scale, and an empty track means undisclosed rather than zero. The striking point is how little scale data is available. Utrecht publishes a fleet number; the three commercial propositions do not. That makes it impossible to establish from public evidence whether European V2G is moving from launch to material scale.11

Europe deserves more time than those numbers initially suggest. BMW, Renault and Volkswagen have only just launched these products; a count taken in 2026 is a count taken on the release date. The issue is not whether they have failed. It is how much time Europe has to let them mature.

It is also worth being precise about what the standards do. ISO 15118-20 specifies the communication layer. It does not mandate a bidirectional inverter, a metering arrangement, a settlement route or a supplier willing to buy — which is why Germany needed a separate legal change before bidirectional charging became commercially possible there at all.12 An agreed handshake between parties who have agreed nothing else is not yet interoperability.

The question is not whether Europe needs V2G. It is whose architecture it ends up using.

Which is where Britain becomes consequential without anything melodramatic being claimed. If Chinese manufacturers and charging firms do not solve British kerbside V2G, nothing follows. If they do, Europe has a live mass-market reference model sitting across the Channel while it is still trying to close its own storage and flexibility gap — and the choice stops being whether to have V2G and becomes whether to keep scaling vertical national products or adopt a proven one.

03India

Why buy the battery twice?

India’s EV market is overwhelmingly two- and three-wheelers. That fact is usually offered as the opportunity. It is actually the setup.

India’s electricity problem is increasingly one of timing rather than simple annual shortage. Solar is adding abundant daytime generation while the evening peak continues to rise: the evening net-load ramp has grown from about 27 GW in 2023 to about 65 GW in 2026, and across April and May 2026 the grid fell short on 36 of 61 days at the non-solar peak against 6 of 61 at the solar peak.14 The Central Electricity Authority sees a need for 208 GWh of battery storage by 2029–30.13 At the same time electricity demand is forecast to grow around 6.4% a year to 2030, with new loads including data-centre capacity rising from 2.2 GW to about 12 GW.15

The answer that does not work

Of 2.3 million EVs sold in India in 2025, two-wheelers were 57% and three-wheelers 35% — 92% of the market.16 Their batteries are generally measured in single-digit kilowatt-hours: 3.4 to 4 kWh for a typical electric scooter, 5 to 6 kWh for a three-wheeler pack. That makes them excellent mobility assets and potentially useful household-scale energy assets, but a much less obvious route to hundreds of gigawatt-hours of national storage.

Which is exactly why the space for the car is open.

The market nobody has taken

India sold about 4.7 million passenger vehicles in FY26, of which 198,224 were electric — 4.3% penetration, up from 2.7% a year earlier.17 The market is expected to reach around 6 million by 2030, with the government targeting 30% EV penetration and independent forecasts closer to 20%. A car pack is 50 to 80 kWh: fifteen to twenty times a scooter’s.

At 20% of a 6 million-unit market and a 50 kWh pack, each year of passenger-EV sales would add around 60 GWh of battery nameplate to the Indian economy — nearly 30% of the CEA’s 208 GWh requirement, every year. That is not 60 GWh of available grid storage. But it is too large an adjacent battery estate to ignore, and it accumulates.

The value pool Europe does not have

What makes India distinctive is not the grid arithmetic. It is that India already has two large and increasingly overlapping battery investments: household and business backup, and batteries bought for mobility. The question is whether the passenger EV can be designed so that some households no longer need to buy both separately. The inverter-battery market runs at about $1.02bn a year and the diesel generator market at about $1.33bn, some 60% of the latter in standby duty with residential the fastest-growing segment.19

These are overlapping value pools, not substitution. Not every EV replaces a generator, not every household owns one, and the vehicle is unavailable to the house whenever it is being driven.

The opportunity this creates is not a car with a feature. It is an oven-ready energy asset: car, bidirectional charger, home-islanding capability, software and — when regulation permits — a grid-flexibility contract.

There is an important technical qualification. A car capable of exporting electricity is not automatically capable of keeping a house running through an outage: the home installation must safely disconnect from the grid and create its own local supply. Any claim that an EV can replace household backup therefore depends on the charger and the electrical installation as well as the vehicle.20

Two large qualifications

The first is that vehicle batteries are not equivalent to stationary BESS. They are unavailable when driven, they degrade on cycling, they sit behind a distribution network that has to accept export, and they deliver a fraction of nameplate into any given hour. None of this means India’s future cars become a 200 GWh virtual battery. They will not — availability alone makes that impossible. The more interesting point is that even conservative access to a rapidly growing battery estate could become material, while providing household resilience before grid export is even permitted.

The second is regulatory, and it is the nearer blocker. Vehicle-to-load and limited vehicle-to-home are permitted in India; grid export is not. The Ministry of Power asked the CEA to frame reverse-charging guidelines in March 2023, and the grid code still needs amending.18 Nothing above the household meter happens until that is done. Notably, the half of the proposition that matters most to a household needs none of it.

The economics are also more subtle than “free storage”. The motorist funds the expensive part — the battery — because they wanted a car. The energy system would pay only the incremental cost of making some fraction of it dispatchable, alongside charger infrastructure, grid reinforcement, aggregation and the stationary storage that vehicles cannot replace. That is a materially different proposition from procuring the same energy as BESS, and a materially weaker one than the arithmetic alone suggests.

India’s passenger EV market is not an open field in the ordinary sense. Tata, JSW MG and Mahindra between them hold about 87% of it, and Tata alone around 39%.17 What is unoccupied is narrower and more specific: no incumbent has assembled an integrated passenger-EV plus home-backup plus grid-flexibility proposition. That is the white space, and it is a product decision rather than a market position.

05What would change the picture

Seven things worth watching

Each is public, dated and checkable, and several would overturn something argued above.

A UK local authority specifies bidirectional capability in a kerbside charging tender

This would move faster than anything else here. LEVI-funded procurement is where the one-way decision is currently being taken by default rather than on the merits, and a single specification would tell you that somebody has connected the two infrastructure decisions Britain is making separately.

A second Chinese manufacturer ships a V2G-capable car into the UK or EU

Today only BYD’s Dolphin qualifies, which is why we treat the convergence argument as a possibility rather than a trend. Two or three would make it a strategy worth planning against.

European programmes begin publishing fleet numbers

Three of the four we examined publish nothing at all. Disclosure alone would remove the evidence gap that currently makes it impossible to judge, in either direction, whether European V2G is moving from launch to material scale.

A European V2G programme passes 50,000 vehicles

A different question from disclosure, and the one that would demonstrate actual scale rather than merely reveal it. It would also be the first evidence that a vertical product can grow into something resembling a market.

One V2G tariff accepts vehicles from three or more unrelated manufacturers

The cleanest available test of whether interoperability is real or merely standardised on paper, and the point at which a European V2G market rather than a set of vertical products can be said to exist.

India notifies reverse-charging guidelines and amends the grid code

Asked for in March 2023 and still outstanding. Until it lands, everything above the household meter in India remains theory — though the household half does not depend on it.

A manufacturer homologates a bidirectional passenger car for the Indian market

Cheap to design in now and expensive to retrofit across a national parc later. Whoever does it first sets what an Indian electric car is assumed to include.

In closing

The question V2G has been asked for a decade

V2G may have spent a decade being asked the wrong question. Selling a few kilowatt-hours from a car back to the grid is not particularly exciting, and in many cases the economics still are not.

But Britain’s kerbside problem, Europe’s storage requirement and India’s need for both resilience and flexibility are not small problems. In each case the expensive part — the battery — is increasingly being bought for another purpose, by someone who wanted a car. Whatever the energy system eventually pays, it is not paying for that.

Perhaps the interesting question is not what the electricity in a parked car is worth. It is what problems we decide to let that battery solve.
